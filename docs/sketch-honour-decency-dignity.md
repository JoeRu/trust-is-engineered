## Sketch: Honour, Decency and Dignity in the Evidence Model

> **DRAFT – SKETCH.** Idea note, not peer reviewed and not empirically validated. Not yet part of the working paper; nothing here may be cited as a statement of the model. Content, numbering and references will change.

*Planned companion paper to “Trust is Engineered” v0.7 (paper as of 22 October 2026) · Sketch as of 31 October 2026 · Derived from the model, not yet empirically tested · Section references refer to v0.7*

**Short question.** Can the everyday virtues honour, decency and dignity be read as quantities of the evidence model? Working proposal: **honour** is reputation on the integrity dimension, **decency** is shown benevolence plus adherence to norms of interaction, and **dignity** is a floor that does not depend on evidence. The first two can be earned and lost; the third cannot. All three readings are proposals of this sketch, not findings. Applied to right-wing populism (E6), the sketch proposes that the universal parts of these values recede while their in-group parts are emphasised, and that pointing out violations helps only if it is costly, credible and itself respects dignity.

### E0 Research questions

1. **RQ1.** Why do honour, decency and dignity seem to recede in public debate with the rise of right-wing populist parties?
2. **RQ2.** Can extremist positions be countered by pointing out that they violate these values?

Following Case Study 1, the sketch analyses techniques and mechanisms, not actors or voters. Sections E1–E4 provide the conceptual mapping; E6 applies it to the two questions.

### E1 Starting point: the author's intuition

The starting intuition, in paraphrase:

| Term | Intuition |
| --- | --- |
| Honour | "I always tell the truth; it matters to me that my person produces strong positive evidence." |
| Decency | "I mean well towards you and all of you." |
| Dignity | "I stand by the rightness of my actions and words." |

### E2 Proposed mapping to model quantities

| Term | Proposed reading | Model quantity | Section |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Honour (1) – truthfulness | trait on the integrity dimension | integrity, "honesty" | 2.4 |
| Honour (2) – standing | evidence that *others* hold about A on the integrity dimension, i.e. reputation | (r, s) on edges X→A, propagation | 4.1, 4.6 |
| Decency | shown benevolence, plus respect for norms of interaction | benevolence ("benevolence, respect"); partly integrity ("rules") | 2.4 |
| Dignity (intuition) | owning one's actions and words, including the negative ones | integrity, "responsibility" | 2.4 |
| Dignity (literature) | inherent worth that is neither earned nor forfeited | **no counterpart yet**: a floor below which the treatment of a person does not fall, independent of E | – |

**Honour must be split.** The intuition joins two things: a trait of A (truthfulness) and the evidence account others keep about A (standing). The model keeps both apart: a trait generates events, and events become evidence on someone else's edge. Honour in the sense of standing is therefore not a property of A but of the edges pointing at A.

**Why honour loss feels total.** Integrity violations weigh more heavily than competence errors (4.4) and integrity evidence transfers broadly across contexts (4.5). If honour is reputation on the integrity dimension, one exposed lie should lower trust in many contexts at once. This is a derivation from the model, not an observation (hypothesis HE1).

**Dignity: the intuition and the literature diverge.** The intuition ("I stand by what I did") already exists in the model as *responsibility*, a trait on the integrity dimension (2.4). In the philosophical and cultural-psychological sense, dignity means something else: a worth that does not depend on what others observe. Leung & Cohen (2011) contrast three cultural logics of self-worth: in dignity cultures worth is inherent, in honour cultures it is claimed and must be defended in the eyes of others, in face cultures it is conferred within a hierarchy (new, to be checked; summarised from secondary knowledge, abstract and full text not yet read). **The model currently has no quantity that is not driven by evidence.** Dignity would be a new element: a lower bound on respectful treatment that holds even at low E. It belongs to the decision layer (6), not to the evidence layer (4).

### E4 Open questions

1. Is honour (standing) only reputation, or also A's own estimate of it (a second-order belief)?
2. Where does decency end and integrity begin? The term "respect" sits in benevolence, "rules" in integrity (2.4).
3. Does a dignity floor conflict with the threshold rule (6.2)? It must not grant permissions; it can only constrain treatment.
4. How do these terms map to agents (10)? An agent can be "honest" in the integrity sense; whether dignity applies is a normative, not a model question.
5. The project rules forbid scoring persons for decisions by third parties. An "honour score" must not result from this sketch.

### E5 Working definitions from the literature

All entries below are taken from secondary summaries found in a literature search on 31 October 2026 (new, secondary citation). None has been read in full. They mark positions, not settled definitions.

**Honour**

- *Honour as self-worth plus recognition.* Pitt-Rivers (1965): honour joins a person's estimate of their own worth to the recognition of that worth by others. Closest to the split in E2: trait plus standing.
- *Honour as a right to respect.* Stewart (1994) rejects equating honour with reputation, a feeling or integrity. Honour is a claim to be treated with respect. This challenges the reading "honour = reputation".
- *Honour as a multifaceted construct.* Cross et al. (2014) find shared dimensions of self-respect, moral behaviour and social status/respect in Turkish and Northern US prototypes. Rodriguez Mosquera (2016) separates moral, family, masculine and feminine honour. For the model, **moral honour** is the relevant facet.
- *Honour as a cultural logic.* Leung & Cohen (2011): in honour cultures worth is claimed and defended, in dignity cultures it is inherent, in face cultures it is conferred within a hierarchy; individuals vary within each culture.
- *Honour as costly signal.* Karimi-Malekabadi & Oyserman (2025) theorise honour as costly moral action that can signal trustworthiness. This is the closest link to the evidence model: honourable action as a costly, hence credible, event.

**Dignity**

- *Dignity versus price.* Kant (1785): what has a price can be replaced by an equivalent; what has dignity is beyond price. Supports the reading "not driven by evidence".
- *Recognition respect versus appraisal respect.* Darwall (1977), read in full on 2026-10-31. Recognition respect "consists, most generally, in a disposition to weigh appropriately in one's deliberations some feature of the thing in question and to act accordingly" (p. 38); in its moral form a fact about the object places "moral limits" on what one may do (p. 40). Appraisal respect "consists in an attitude of positive appraisal of that person either as a person or as engaged in some particular pursuit" (p. 38); it "is not owed to everyone, for it may or may not be merited" (p. 45) and "admits of degree" (p. 44). For persons as such there are no degrees of recognition respect (p. 46), and recognition respect "is not necessarily to give him *credit* for anything in particular" (p. 46). The distinction removes the puzzle that all persons are owed respect and yet deserve "more or less respect by virtue of their personal characteristics" (p. 46). Darwall does not develop honour, dignity or decency as concepts: "dignity" appears once, as "a being with a certain moral status or dignity" in the discussion of self-respect (p. 47); "honor" only in "honor among thieves" (p. 41). Self-respect also comes in both kinds; appraisal self-respect rests on character and differs from self-esteem (pp. 47–48). **For the sketch:** dignity calls for recognition respect (no degrees, independent of E); honour and decency, as far as they are earned, call for appraisal respect (graded, dependent on E). This mapping is the sketch's, not Darwall's.
- *Dignity as standing to make claims.* Darwall (2006): dignity is the equal standing to make claims within relations of mutual accountability.
- *Several meanings.* Rosen (2012) distinguishes dignity as rank, as intrinsic value, as dignified behaviour and as respectful treatment. The intuition in E1 ("I stand by my actions") is closest to *dignified behaviour*. Waldron (2012) reads modern human dignity as high rank extended to everyone.
- *Measurement.* Workplace Dignity Scale (Thomas & Lucas 2019; validity of its two factors questioned by a later validation study) and a general Dignity Scale (Pirson et al. 2023). Both measure *perceived* dignity, not intrinsic worth.

**Decency (Anstand)**

- *Decency as non-humiliation.* Margalit (1996), not read in full; passages seen via the publisher page, Google Books preview snippets and reviews. "A civilized society is one whose members do not humiliate one another, while a decent society is one in which the institutions do not humiliate people" (p. 1). Humiliation is "any sort of behavior or condition that constitutes a sound reason for a person to consider his or her self-respect injured" (p. 9). According to a review (Univ. of Utah collection), Margalit names three forms: treating people as non-human, taking away control over central parts of their life, and rejection from an encompassing group; and three ways to ground respect for persons (positive, sceptical, negative); he favours the negative one, which does not justify respect but condemns humiliation as mental cruelty. The account is normative: what counts is a *sound reason* to feel humiliated, not the feeling itself. This fits the dignity floor in E2: decency is the *practice* that keeps that floor.
- *Civility.* Calhoun (2000): civility displays respect, tolerance or considerateness through social norms, which may themselves be flawed.
- *Common decency.* Calhoun (2016): socially expected moral gestures, neither strict duties nor pure gifts.
- *Incivility spiral.* Andersson & Pearson (1999): small breaches of respect can escalate.
- Note: German *Anstand* covers both manners/propriety and moral decency. There is no exact English equivalent.

**Revised proposal after the search**

| Term | Revised reading | Respect type (Darwall) | Depends on evidence? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Honour (moral) | costly, visible adherence to truth and principles; standing on the integrity dimension | appraisal respect | yes |
| Decency | treating others with respect and without humiliation; benevolence plus norms | appraisal respect (when shown), owed as minimum | partly |
| Dignity | standing that is owed, not earned; floor of treatment | recognition respect | no |

### E6 Applying the mapping to RQ1 and RQ2

All findings below are from abstracts and secondary summaries (new, secondary citation); DOIs confirmed 2026-10-31. Model links are derivations of this sketch.

**RQ1: the values do not simply recede – they are re-weighted and narrowed.** Three mechanisms from the literature:

| Mechanism | Literature | Reading in the model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Norm erosion.* A signal that a candidate is locally popular makes people more willing to express xenophobic views in public and lowers sanctions against those who do; private behaviour does not change. | Bursztyn, Egorov & Fiorin (2020, read in full: Pittsburgh, 2018, N = 1,600 and 1,830; public donations to an anti-immigration group 33 % vs 20 %, private 31 % vs 30 %); Valentim (2024), with the AfD as a central case | Shift of the descriptive norm, i.e. of group prior G and beliefs (3.3, 3.4); "norms beat knowledge" (7.5). The norm loses its social enforcement, not its private endorsement: popularity "does not make these participants more xenophobic, but instead makes those who were already xenophobic more comfortable expressing their xenophobic views in public" (Bursztyn et al. 2020, p. 3525). |
| *Style of the "low".* Populist performance uses "bad manners" to mark distance from elites. | Moffitt & Tormey (2014); Ostiguy (2017); Stein, Luebke & Engelmann (2025, German experiment: colloquial style raised perceived authenticity) | Incivility as a signal of authenticity, i.e. evidence on the integrity dimension ("says what he thinks"). **Counter-evidence:** Frimer & Skitka (2018) find that incivility lowers approval even among a politician's own base. The signal value is contested. |
| *Narrowing of dignity and honour to the in-group.* Perceived loss of status and recognition; demand for dignity turns into ressentiment and collective narcissism. | Gidron & Hall (2017); Lamont (2018); Salmela & von Scheve (2017); Golec de Zavala & Keenan (2021); Fukuyama (2018, non-peer-reviewed book) | Honour is not abandoned but redefined as **group honour** (appraisal respect for "us"). Dignity loses its universality: recognition respect becomes conditional on membership. In Darwall's terms this is the decisive shift – a graded, earned respect replaces an ungraded, owed one. |

**Working answer to RQ1 (hypothesis, not finding).** What recedes is the *universal* part (dignity as recognition respect for everyone, decency as non-humiliation of everyone). The *particular* part (honour of one's own group, dignity claims of one's own group) is emphasised. Supporters may experience themselves as defenders of honour and dignity – of their own group.

**RQ2: calling out violations can work, but only under conditions – and it can backfire.**

| Finding | Literature | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Criticism by other MPs lowers the chance that a candidate is chosen by 2.9 (verbal criticism) to 7.4 (call for expulsion) percentage points; criticism from the candidate's own party adds only 1.8 points. Voters did not punish authoritarian conduct more than ordinary misconduct. | Hobolt & Osnabrügge (2025, read in full: England, 2021, conjoint, N = 4,012); Munger (2017, in-group messenger on Twitter) | **Costliness matters more than the messenger:** actions (refusal to cooperate, expulsion) work better than words. Value violations against democracy are not perceived as especially grave – pointing them out is not self-evidently effective. |
| Voters penalise undemocratic candidates, but competence and partisanship partly offset the penalty. | Graham & Svolik (2020); Frederiksen (2022) | Value violations compete with other evidence; pointing them out alone is not decisive. |
| Arguments in the recipient's moral values persuade better than in the speaker's. | Feinberg & Willer (2015, 2019); **but** no consistent effects in Hawkins et al. (2026) | "Reframing" is promising, not a reliable recipe. |
| Non-judgmental, narrative conversations durably reduce exclusionary attitudes; arguments alone showed no effect. | Broockman & Kalla (2016); Kalla & Broockman (2020) | Respectful form is part of the effect. |
| Of 25 treatments, six reduced support for undemocratic practices, among them correcting misperceptions and highlighting democratic collapse. | Voelkel et al. (2024) | Pointing out consequences can work; reducing animosity alone does not suffice. |
| Perceived cultural distance and condescension correlate with populist attitudes. | Noordzij, de Koster & van der Waal (2021) | **Backfire risk:** condemnation of voters is received as contempt. |
| Exclusion (cordon sanitaire) has mixed electoral effects; a German 2025 experiment found no effect of firewall signals. | van Spanje & van der Brug (2009); Fahey & Alarian (2025) | Exclusion of parties is not a value argument and is not shown to persuade. |
| Prebunking improves recognition of manipulation techniques. | Roozenbeek et al. (2022) | Complements Case Study 1 (techniques, not values). |

**Working answer to RQ2 (hypothesis, not finding).** Pointing out value violations is not effective by itself: in the one full-text study, voters did not punish authoritarian conduct more than ordinary misconduct, and verbal criticism had the smallest effect (Hobolt & Osnabrügge 2025). It can help under three conditions: (1) it is **costly and credible** – consequences such as refusing cooperation weigh more than words, and who speaks matters less than what is done; (2) it is itself **dignity-consistent** – it names conduct and harm, not the worth of the people; (3) it appeals to values the audience holds – honour and dignity are such values. Whether an honour frame works better than a decency or democracy frame has not been tested (research gap, HE8). It backfires if it is received as humiliation (Margalit): then it delivers negative benevolence evidence on the edge from voters to the critics and widens the calibration gap towards institutions (Γ < 0, Case Study 1).
