## 1. Scope and method

**Question.** For each contribution that Paper 1 claims (paper; long version, Chapters 2–6, 8, 11, 12), which prior work comes closest, and what remains once that work is taken into account?

**Starting set.** All references in Chapter 16 of the long version that concern the core model: the sub-lists "Concept of trust and models", "Formal and computational models", "Asymmetry, violation and repair", "Repetition and familiarity", and the parts of the other lists that Paper 1 uses. These keep their markers from the long version unchanged. Most entries in Chapter 16 have no marker; according to the legend of Chapter 16, most of them have not been checked against the full text either.

**Search.** Search date was 18 October 2026. Tools were the Crossref REST API (`api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=…`, plus `works/<DOI>` for abstracts), the OpenAlex API (abstracts and citation counts), the Semantic Scholar Graph API (citation counts), a general web search, and publisher or repository pages (SpringerLink, PubMed, JASSS, institutional repositories). Search terms combined the requested lines of work with the model's terms: "computational trust and reputation model review", "beta reputation forgetting", "evidence-based trust certainty", "propagation of trust and distrust", "inaccurate reputation sources", "trust and distrust distinct constructs", "trust in automation appropriate reliance", "trust calibration measurement survey", "trust dynamics Bayesian inference", "trustworthiness dynamic belief reinforcement learning", "DeGroot consensus", "bounded confidence opinion dynamics", "naive learning social networks", "persuasion bias repetition", "naive herding redundancy neglect", "correlation neglect", "referential theory truth effect", "formal models of source reliability", "attacks on reputation systems", "social engineering attack framework / ontology / definition", "scam victims principles".

**Inclusion criteria.** (a) The work is a classic of a requested line of work, or a peer-reviewed survey of it, or (b) it is peer-reviewed and directly overlaps a formal element of Paper 1: the opinion triple, evidence accounts with decay, source dependence, the normative/descriptive split and Γ, the decision thresholds, or attacks read as operations on model quantities. Preference went to well-cited work (citation counts from OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar) and to work from 2015–2026.

**Limits, stated plainly.**
- No full text of a new reference was read. Each new work is described only as far as its abstract or publisher description goes. These works are marked "(new, secondary citation)".
- For two works no abstract could be read; their existence and bibliographic data were confirmed through Crossref only, and they are marked "(new, to be checked)": de Visser et al. 2020 (publisher and repository pages were unreachable or blocked) and Kish 1965 (book; only reviews of it are indexed in Crossref).
- Some pages were blocked or unreachable (ScienceDirect, ACM Digital Library, Taylor & Francis, Southampton ePrints, TU Delft repository). Abstracts were taken from Crossref or OpenAlex instead, or from a repository copy for Jøsang, Ismail & Boyd 2007.
- One statement about an existing reference was checked against its full text for this document: the Beta Reputation System (Jøsang & Ismail 2002) contains a forgetting factor (its Section 2.6) and a discounting of feedback by the reputation of the rater (its Section 2.5). Its marker in Chapter 16 is left unchanged, because changing it is the author's decision; the check is reported in Section 3.
- The search was not systematic in the sense of PRISMA. Work in German, work in non-indexed venues and grey literature on fraud practice were not covered. No search was made for very recent preprints (2025–2026) on calibration of trust in LLM agents; those belong to Paper 3.

## 2. Lines of prior work

### 2.1 Computational trust and reputation models

**What the line does.** Marsh (1994) gave an early formalization of trust as a computational concept. Reputation systems aggregate ratings into scores that support later decisions. Jøsang, Ismail & Boyd (2007) describe them as collaborative sanctioning mechanisms and survey existing and proposed systems for online service provision (new). Reviews of computational trust and reputation models for multi-agent systems classify the models along several dimensions (Sabater & Sierra 2005, new). A later review notes a growth of cognitive approaches that treat trust and reputation as mental attitudes and not only as final numerical values (Pinyol & Sabater-Mir 2013, new). The Beta Reputation System (Jøsang & Ismail 2002) represents ratings as positive and negative evidence counts of a beta distribution; it already contains a forgetting factor that gives old feedback less weight, and a discounting of feedback by the reputation of the rater. Subjective Logic (Jøsang 2016) generalizes this to opinions (b, d, u, a) with operators for discounting and fusion. Wang & Singh (2010, new) define trust as certainty in the belief that a partner is trustworthy, compute this certainty from evidence over a probability distribution of positive outcomes, and support discounting and combining trust reports. They show that certainty grows with the amount of evidence and falls with conflict in the evidence. TRAVOS (Teacy et al. 2006, new) estimates trust probabilistically from past interactions and uses third-party reputation when direct experience is lacking, explicitly handling reputation information that may be inaccurate. Guha et al. (2004, new) build a framework of trust propagation schemes in a directed rating network and, by their own account, were the first to incorporate distrust into computational trust propagation. Christianson & Harbison (1997) argue that trust is not transitive in general.

**Relation to Paper 1.** Paper 1 **adopts** this line for its formal core and should say so without hedging. The opinion triple (3.1), the evidence accounts r and s with base rate a and prior strength W (4.1), the forgetting factor λ (4.3) and the discounting operator along referral chains (4.6) are taken from the Beta Reputation System and Subjective Logic. The long version names them as sources ("follows Subjective Logic", "corresponds to the Beta Reputation System"). It does not yet say that the Beta Reputation System already contains forgetting and rater discounting. Section 4.3 therefore reads as if temporal decay were added by the model. **Closest prior work:** Jøsang & Ismail 2002 and Jøsang 2016 for the formal core; Wang & Singh 2010 for "certainty from evidence"; Guha et al. 2004 for propagating trust and distrust as separate quantities in a graph.

**What Paper 1 adds.** Within this line, the additions are not new operators. They are (a) an event weight g± that multiplies source independence ϱ, diagnosticity q, dimension weight w±, context relevance r(e,c) and valence v (4.3); (b) a context triple (task domain × vulnerability type × relationship type) and the four trustworthiness dimensions as indices of each edge (3.2, 2.4); (c) closed-form readings of decay: "trust decays to ignorance, not to zero", the half-life t½ and the lower bound u_min at a constant evidence rate (4.3); (d) a second, descriptive account kept next to the normative one (Section 2.6 below). Reputation-system research usually aims at an accurate score for decisions. Paper 1 instead uses the evidence algebra as a normative reference against which human trust is compared. That difference in use is real but has to be argued, not assumed.

### 2.2 Socio-cognitive trust theory

**What the line does.** Castelfranchi & Falcone (2010) treat trust as a mental attitude made of beliefs and goals about the trustee and as a decision to rely on the trustee. Pinyol & Sabater-Mir (2013, new) adopt Castelfranchi's view that trusting someone means deciding to rely on them as the basis of their objective definition of trust. Chen (2021) adds a goal to the trust relation, R(A, B, φ, G), and separates reliance from trust: a malfunction leads to disappointment, a breach of trust to a feeling of betrayal.

**Relation to Paper 1.** Paper 1 **overlaps** with this line in separating evaluation from decision. The five separated quantities in 2.1 and the three questions of 6.1 are in the spirit of the socio-cognitive decomposition. Paper 1 **differs** in that it does not model beliefs and goals as explicit mental states of the trustor about the trustee. Its beliefs 𝓑 are filters on a, W and v (5.3), not a belief-goal structure. The long version already concedes, in Chen's terms, that the model mainly describes calibrated reliance (2.5, 15.3). **Closest prior work:** Castelfranchi & Falcone 2010. A reviewer from the agents community will ask why the socio-cognitive decomposition is not used as the descriptive account. Paper 1 should answer briefly: the descriptive account has to be estimated from behavior with few parameters, which a full belief-goal model does not allow. That is an argument from identifiability (15.3), not a claim of superiority.

### 2.3 Organizational trust and the trust–distrust distinction

**What the line does.** Mayer et al. (1995) define trust as willingness to be vulnerable, separate trust from risk taking, and give the ability–benevolence–integrity factors. Rousseau et al. (1998) give the cross-disciplinary definition that the long version adopts. Lewicki et al. (1998) argue that trust and distrust are separate dimensions that can coexist. McKnight et al. (1998) model initial trust formation; McKnight, Choudhury & Kacmar (2002) give measures of trust in e-commerce. McKnight & Chervany (2001, new) propose a cohesive set of constructs (disposition, institution-based trust and distrust, trusting and distrusting beliefs and intentions) and define distrust as separate from and opposite to trust, with both operating at the same time. Dimoka (2010, new) reports in an fMRI study of online seller profiles that trust and distrust activate different brain areas and have different effects. Reimann et al. (2017) report different origins: trust is partly heritable, distrust is not.

**Relation to Paper 1.** Paper 1 **adopts** the definition and dimensions and **formalizes** the trust–distrust distinction by reading Lewicki's two dimensions as b and d of an opinion. The long version uses this to separate ignorance (low b, low d) from ambivalence (high b, high d) (2.2, Figure 1). **Overlap risk.** The idea that trust and distrust are distinct and that one scalar hides the difference is old: Lewicki et al. 1998 in organizational research, McKnight & Chervany 2001 in information systems, and Guha et al. 2004 computationally. One mismatch should be stated openly. In Subjective Logic, b + d + u = 1. High b and high d at once therefore imply low u, and "ambivalence" in the opinion triple means "much evidence of both signs". Lewicki et al. (1998) and McKnight & Chervany (2001) treat trust and distrust as separate constructs; whether their "high trust, high distrust" corresponds to "much mixed evidence" is an interpretation. That is exactly the long version's open question in 15.5 ("two dimensions or two readings of the same evidence?"). Paper 1 should present the mapping as a modeling choice, not as a formal rendering of Lewicki's theory.

### 2.4 Trust calibration and appropriate reliance

**What the line does.** Lee & See (2004) review trust in automation and frame the goal as appropriate reliance: trust should match the capabilities of the automation. Their dimensions performance, process and purpose are already mapped in the long version (2.4). Parasuraman & Riley (1997) describe misuse and disuse of automation, that is, over- and underreliance. Hoff & Bashir (2015, new) review 127 studies and organize the variability of trust in automation into three layers: dispositional, situational and learned trust. De Visser et al. (2020, new, to be checked) propose a theory of longitudinal trust calibration in human–robot teams; only title and metadata were seen. Wischnewski, Krämer & Müller (2023, new) review 96 publications that aimed to calibrate users' trust in automated systems, summarize how calibration is measured and how interventions are designed, and structure the interventions along four dimensions. Jacovi et al. (2021) call human–AI trust warranted only if it is caused by actual trustworthiness. Guo & Yang (2021, new) model trust dynamics in a human–robot task with Bayesian inference. They start from the premise that a rational human updates trust after observing performance, fit a personalized prediction model, and identify three types of trust dynamics ("Bayesian decision maker", "oscillator", "disbeliever").

**Relation to Paper 1.** This is the line against which the **calibration gap Γ** will be judged, and it is the largest overlap risk after Jøsang. Lee & See (2004) and the calibration literature already define good trust as trust matched to trustworthiness and name both error directions. Paper 1's Γ > 0 (false trust) and Γ < 0 (false distrust) restate this distinction between over- and undertrust. **What Paper 1 adds** is narrower and formal. (a) The reference against which trust is calibrated is not the true reliability of the trustee, which is often unknown, but a *normative evidence account*: what the available evidence justifies after weighting by independence D and diagnosticity q. (b) Both accounts run on the same event stream, so Γ is defined per edge, context and dimension and at every point in time. (c) The descriptive account differs from the normative one by named, separately parameterized terms: the familiarity term φ(1−m)ln(1+R), the neglect of D and belief-colored valence. Each mechanism of miscalibration is therefore a separate model quantity. (d) Γ is read both as an attack surface and as a training target (4.8). **Closest prior work:** Lee & See 2004 (concept), Wischnewski et al. 2023 (measurement), Guo & Yang 2021 (formal: a rational Bayesian updater as reference, human deviations as types). In Guo & Yang (2021), the reference is a rational Bayesian updater and individual deviations are classified into types. That is structurally close to the normative and descriptive accounts. Paper 1 should cite it and explain the difference: Paper 1 treats evidence from people and sources with dependence and diagnosticity, while Guo & Yang treat observed performance of a robot. Hoff & Bashir's three layers overlap with the four levels of Chapter 5 (disposition, beliefs, learning parameters, momentary state). Paper 1 should cite the correspondence instead of presenting the levels as its own.

### 2.5 Learning-rate, Bayesian and opinion-dynamics models of belief and trust

**What the line does.** Learning models: Chang et al. (2010, new) use a repeated trust game and reinforcement-learning models and propose that trustworthiness is a belief about the probability of reciprocation, which starts from implicit initial judgments and is updated by experience. Behrens et al. (2007) show that learning rates track environmental volatility. Opinion dynamics: DeGroot (1974, new) models how a group reaches consensus by repeatedly pooling individual subjective distributions or point estimates. Friedkin & Johnsen (1990) add anchoring to initial opinions. Hegselmann & Krause (2002, new) analyze, in one framework, the classical consensus model, the Friedkin–Johnsen variant and a nonlinear bounded-confidence model in which agents only take into account opinions close enough to their own, and ask when consensus, polarization or fragmentation arise. Golub & Jackson (2010, new) show that naive averaging in a network converges to the truth in large societies if and only if the influence of the most influential agent vanishes, and that prominent groups can obstruct this. Bayesian epistemology: Merdes, von Sydow & Hahn (2021, new) compare Bayesian models of source reliability and conclude that important normative questions remain unresolved.

**Relation to Paper 1.** On learning rates, Paper 1 **overlaps** with known results and should say so. The effect of one event on E with learning rate g/(n+g) (4.4) is the standard property of beta updating, and the long version itself names Bush–Mosteller and Rescorla–Wagner. "Buffering" and "structural asymmetry" follow from it and are not new. The new part is the dimension-dependent weights w± (H5a), and those rest on the cited asymmetry literature, not on the formalism. Chang et al. (2010) are the closest empirical-computational precedent for "prior from first impressions, then learning from experience" (4.2, 5.2) and are missing from the long version.

On opinion dynamics, Paper 1 **differs in object**. These models update opinions about a state of the world through mutual influence. Paper 1 updates trust in a source from evidence about that source, and uses group anchoring only as a prior (3.3, 3.4: Friedkin–Johnsen as a possible formal representation). Network-level prediction V21 ("trust recovers more slowly after a shock than it fell") and the echo-chamber reading belong to the territory of these models. A reviewer will expect V21 to be stated against DeGroot-type and bounded-confidence baselines, or at least to name them as the alternative model class for simulation scenario 7 and V21. Golub & Jackson (2010) are the formal counterpart of the long version's remark that the wisdom of crowds holds only without mutual influence (Lorenz et al. 2011). Merdes et al. (2021) matter for the normative account: if normative Bayesian source-reliability models do not agree with one another, "the normative account" is one of several possible standards. That supports the limitation "normative standard" (15.3) and should be cited there.

### 2.6 Correlated sources, illusory truth and naive social learning

**What the line does.** Experiments show that many people treat correlated information as independent, so that their beliefs overreact to well-connected sources (Enke & Zimmermann 2019). DeMarzo, Vayanos & Zwiebel (2003, new) propose a boundedly rational model of persuasion bias: agents fail to adjust for repetitions of information they receive in a network, and beliefs converge to a weighted average whose weights measure social influence. Eyster & Rabin (2010, new) model naive herders who believe that each predecessor's action reflects only that predecessor's private information; they over-weight early signals and can herd on wrong actions with full confidence. The illusory truth effect (Hasher et al. 1977) is small and robust (Ye et al. 2026) and grows with diminishing returns in repetitions (Hassan & Barber 2021). Unkelbach & Rom (2017, new) propose a referential theory: truth judgments depend on the number and coherence of references for a statement in memory, and repetition creates such references. Yousif et al. (2019) show that people distinguish real from apparent consensus poorly. Mattavelli et al. (2025) report that repetition also raises the credibility of the source.

**Relation to Paper 1.** Here Paper 1 **adopts** the empirical findings and **extends** them in one specific way. The descriptive account adds a familiarity term to the normative one, and source dependence D enters only the normative account. Correlation neglect, persuasion bias and naive herding are thereby read as one quantity, the part of Γ due to dependence. That is a clean contribution if presented as an integration. It is not a new mechanism: DeMarzo et al. (2003) and Eyster & Rabin (2010) already model, formally and in a network, the failure to discount repeated or redundant information. Paper 1 must cite them; otherwise the descriptive account looks like a re-invention of persuasion bias.

**Formula attribution.** The effective sample size n_eff = n/(1 + (n−1)D) (4.7) has the form of the design-effect correction for clustered samples known from survey sampling (Kish 1965, new, to be checked; the attribution has to be confirmed against the source). The long version presents it without attribution. Paper 1 should attribute it and state that D plays the role of an intra-cluster correlation. The familiarity term φ(1−m)ln(1+R) follows the logarithmic growth reported by Hassan & Barber (2021). Unkelbach & Rom (2017) offer a different mechanism (coherent references, not fluency), with the prediction that new statements that share references with earlier ones are also judged truer. That is relevant to how R should be counted, since R may need to count shared references and not only identical repetitions.

### 2.7 Formal models of social engineering, deception and trust exploitation

**What the line does.** For reputation systems, Hoffman, Zage & Nita-Rotaru (2009, new) give a framework that decomposes reputation systems and classify attacks by the system components and design choices they target; they also survey defenses. The Sybil attack (Douceur 2002) is the canonical attack on counting identities as independent. For social engineering, Mouton et al. (2014, new) propose an attack framework that builds on Mitnick's attack cycle and a previously proposed ontological model, covering every step from the goal of an attack to its conclusion. Wang, Sun & Zhu (2020, new) trace the conceptual evolution of the term and propose a more precise definition of social engineering in cybersecurity. Stajano & Wilson (2011, new) argue that effective countermeasures depend on first understanding how users fall victim to fraudsters, and derive principles from scam cases (only the one-line abstract was read). Signaling theory (Spence 1973) explains why a signal is credible only when it is costly for deceivers. The liar's dividend (Chesney & Citron 2019) names the blanket denial of true evidence.

**Relation to Paper 1.** The claim "attacks are operations on model quantities" (8.4) **overlaps** with Hoffman et al. (2009) in method: they also classify attacks by the model component they target. Paper 1 differs in what it maps. It maps social-engineering techniques against human trust evaluation (impersonation, authority signals, sock puppets, long trust-building, context switch, time pressure, appeal to beliefs, normalization), and it adds the distinction between *abuse attacks* (exploit P already granted) and *calibration attacks* (enlarge Γ without touching P) (8.2). The social-engineering frameworks found (Mouton et al. 2014; Wang, Sun & Zhu 2020) are process models or definitions; neither abstract describes a quantitative evidence model. On the basis of the abstracts seen, Paper 1's mapping onto a single set of evidence variables appears not to have a direct precedent. This is the weakest-evidenced novelty statement in this document, because the search was not systematic. **Closest prior work:** Hoffman et al. 2009 (method), Douceur 2002 (dependence D as an attack target), Spence 1973 (diagnosticity q).

## 5. Suggested condensed related-work section

> **Related work.** The formal core of the model is adopted from computational trust research. Following Marsh (1994), reputation systems aggregate evidence about a partner into a score for later decisions (Jøsang, Ismail & Boyd 2007; Sabater & Sierra 2005; Pinyol & Sabater-Mir 2013). The Beta Reputation System represents positive and negative evidence as beta counts and already includes a forgetting factor and the discounting of ratings by the reputation of the rater (Jøsang & Ismail 2002). Subjective Logic generalizes this to opinions with trust, distrust, uncertainty and a base rate, together with a discounting operator for chains (Jøsang 2016). Related approaches derive certainty from evidence (Wang & Singh 2010), handle inaccurate reputation sources (Teacy et al. 2006) and propagate distrust separately in a graph (Guha et al. 2004). That trust and distrust are distinct is established in organizational and information-systems research (Lewicki et al. 1998; McKnight & Chervany 2001; Dimoka 2010). The definitions and dimensions used here follow Mayer et al. (1995) and Rousseau et al. (1998). The idea of calibration comes from research on trust in automation: trust should match trustworthiness, and over- and undertrust are both errors (Parasuraman & Riley 1997; Lee & See 2004; Hoff & Bashir 2015; Wischnewski et al. 2023). Trust warranted by actual trustworthiness is the goal (Jacovi et al. 2021), and trust dynamics have been modeled against a rational Bayesian reference (Guo & Yang 2021). Learning models treat trustworthiness as a belief updated from experience (Chang et al. 2010). Opinion-dynamics models describe belief formation through mutual influence (DeGroot 1974; Friedkin & Johnsen 1990; Hegselmann & Krause 2002; Golub & Jackson 2010). People neglect correlation between sources (Enke & Zimmermann 2019), fail to adjust for repeated information (DeMarzo et al. 2003; Eyster & Rabin 2010), and judge repeated statements as truer (Hasher et al. 1977; Unkelbach & Rom 2017). Attacks on reputation systems have been classified by the component they target (Hoffman et al. 2009; Douceur 2002). Social engineering has been described by attack frameworks and definitions (Mouton et al. 2014; Wang, Sun & Zhu 2020). This paper does not propose new operators. It combines these strands into one evidence model with a normative and a descriptive account, defines their difference Γ as the calibration gap, and reads social-engineering techniques as operations on the model's quantities.
