## 1. Scope and method

**Question.** For each contribution of Paper 2, which prior work makes the same or a closely related claim, and what remains once that work is acknowledged?

**Contributions examined** (from the paper and the long version, Chapters 7, 9, 13, 14): (C1) groups act on verification rather than on evaluation; verification is a public good that is undersupplied, with verification coverage C and correlated four-eyes approvals (dependence D); (C2) protection delegation: a miscalibrated assumed detection rate τ_I of the security institution switches off one's own verification (E′), with the tipping prediction V22; (C3) the descriptive norm sets the verification cost K ("norms beat knowledge", H18); (C4) verification is triggered by the action class, not by trust in the sender (threshold θ independent of the person, three separate verifications, H6, H7, H12); (C5) training is evaluated by discriminability and response bias, metrics per team, never per person; (C6) trust dimensions and security objectives form a matrix, competence lacks a CIA counterpart, utility as fourth column, authenticity as anchor (H15, H15a, H15b); (C7) 24 rules with a common justification and a ranking by loss (Chapter 14).

**Where and how searched (18 October 2026).** Crossref REST API (`query.bibliographic`), Semantic Scholar Graph API (abstracts by DOI), web search (general engine), and publisher or institutional repository pages (ACM, IEEE, Emerald, Leiden, Oxford, SINTEF, NDSS, Harvard Berkman, University of Maryland DRUM, JISSec). OpenAlex was not available (daily quota exhausted); direct DOI resolver pages and ScienceDirect could not be fetched in this session, so Elsevier abstracts were taken from Crossref, Semantic Scholar or university repositories. Search terms: phishing training field study / embedded training / meta-analysis of social engineering interventions; compliance budget, rational rejection, security fatigue, awareness campaigns fail; phishing susceptibility model, persuasion principles social engineering; security culture, social proof security, subjective norms security policy compliance; bystander effect cybersecurity, diffusion of responsibility fraud reporting, phishing reporting; risk compensation perceived technical protection, responsibilization; business email compromise review, separation of duty, four-eyes principle; Parker hexad, CIA triad extension, dependability taxonomy, reference model information assurance.

**Inclusion.** Peer-reviewed or widely cited work, classics of each line plus 2015–2026 work, that bears directly on one of C1 to C7. Excluded: vendor reports, preprints without venue (except where they mirror a peer-reviewed version), and works whose existence could not be confirmed with authors, year, title and venue.

**Limits.** Every new entry was seen **only via its abstract, publisher description or repository summary**, never in full text. Statements about new works below therefore repeat only what those abstracts say. Two new entries (Clark & Wilson 1987; Hoffman, Lawson-Jenkins & Blum 2006) could not be checked at abstract level and are marked "to be checked". The search was not systematic (no PRISMA protocol, no backward/forward snowballing beyond the first hits); German-language and practitioner literature (for example BSI awareness material, audit standards on internal control) was not searched. Absence of a work here is not evidence that it does not exist.

## 2. Lines of prior work

### 2.1 Effectiveness of security awareness and phishing training

**What this line does.** Early lab and field studies of embedded training reported that training can help users recognize phishing, while noting that users are not motivated to learn about security, that security is a secondary task, and that it is hard to teach threat recognition without increasing misjudgment of non-threats (Kumaraguru et al. 2010). Large organizational field studies are more sobering: in a spear-phishing experiment with framed embedded training, framing had no significant effect and employees apparently did not read the training material (Caputo et al. 2014); in a 15-month study with more than 14,000 employees, embedded training did not make employees more resilient and could have side effects that increased susceptibility, while warnings worked and crowdsourced reporting was practical (Lain, Kostiainen & Čapkun 2022). A follow-up attributes the effect of embedded training to its nudging (periodic reminder) rather than its content, which employees rarely consume (Lain et al. 2024). The randomized trial with about 19,500 employees cited in the long version found no measurable effect of annual training and only a minimal one of embedded training (Ho et al. 2025). Syntheses diverge in tone: a meta-analysis of 19 experimental studies found large heterogeneity, with intensive and narrowly focused interventions more effective than low-intensity, broad ones (Bullee & Junger 2020); a later meta-analysis of 69 studies reports an overall positive effect (d = 0.75), largest on predictors such as knowledge and attitudes and weakest, with a confidence interval including zero, on measured behavior (Prümmer, van Steen & van den Berg 2025). Lie detection research shows the ceiling of feature-based detection (Bond & DePaulo 2006; Hauch et al. 2016), and skepticism interventions shift response bias (Modirrousta-Galian & Higham 2023; Hoes et al. 2024).

**Relation to Paper 2.** The paper **adopts** the empirical diagnosis (training changes knowledge more than behavior) and offers an explanation for it: training targets evaluation, while verification is set by role, norm and presumed verification by others. That explanation is a hypothesis (H8, H12, V27). **Overlap:** Kumaraguru et al. (2010) already name the false-alarm problem that motivates symmetric practice and measuring discriminability (9.6, principles 3 and 4); Bullee & Junger (2020) already report that narrow focus helps, which is close to "few triggers, fixed response" (9.6, principle 1); Prümmer et al. (2025) already separate predictors from behavior, which is the core of V27. **What remains:** the derivation of these design principles from a single decision rule (6.2, 6.3), and the claim that the missing behavior effect is a group-level phenomenon. Closest prior work: Lain, Kostiainen & Čapkun (2022) and Ho et al. (2025).

### 2.2 Human factors and the economics of usable security

**What this line does.** Herley (2009) argues that rejecting security advice can be individually rational when the cost falls on the user and the benefit on others; Beautement, Sasse & Wonham (2008) describe a limited compliance budget; Stanton et al. (2016) describe security fatigue. Awareness campaigns fail when they only provide information; people must understand and be able to apply advice and be motivated to act (Bada, Sasse & Nurse 2015). The economics of security treats system reliability as a public good with free riding, distinguishing total-effort, weakest-link and best-shot technologies (Varian 2004), and models the choice between self-protection (a public good) and self-insurance (a private good) as a game (Grossklags, Christin & Chuang 2008).

**Relation to Paper 2.** The paper **adopts** compliance budget, rational rejection and security fatigue as constraints on its rules (9.6, principles 2 and 6; S14). **Overlap, strong:** the phrase "verification as a public good" is not new in spirit. Varian (2004) already frames reliability as a public good subject to free riding. The coverage formula C = 1 − (1 − c_n)^n is, in Varian's vocabulary, a best-shot aggregation (one verifier suffices). **What remains:** (a) the unit is a single human verification of a single action, not system-level security investment; (b) the claim that c_n falls with n faster than the exponent compensates is taken from bystander research (Darley & Latané 1968; Fischer et al. 2011), not from rational free riding; (c) the observation that a second verifier with the same information has dependence D close to 1 and adds almost no evidence (7.3, V24, S21). The paper should cite Varian (2004) and Grossklags, Christin & Chuang (2008) when introducing C and say explicitly that it adds a behavioral and an evidential (D) argument to an economic one.

### 2.3 Social-engineering and phishing susceptibility models and taxonomies

**What this line does.** Susceptibility models explain victimization through cognitive processing and, increasingly, through habits: the suspicion, cognition and automaticity model (SCAM) adds preconscious and automatic processes and notes that training effects attenuate as users sink into email habits (Vishwanath, Harrison & Ng 2016). Taxonomies of persuasion merge Cialdini's principles of influence, Gragg's psychological triggers and Stajano and Wilson's scam principles into one list and find them used in specific combinations in phishing emails (Ferreira, Coventry & Lenzini 2015). Business email compromise is reviewed as exploitation of trusted relationships in which social engineering overcomes technical solutions, with a noted research gap on the human elements (Cross & Gillett 2020). The long version adds the fraud triangle (Cressey 1953) and loss data (ACFE 2024; FBI IC3 2024).

**Relation to Paper 2.** The attack table (long version 8.4, rows concerning groups and organizations) **overlaps** with persuasion taxonomies: authority, social proof and urgency appear in both. **What is new** is not the list of techniques but the mapping of each to a model quantity (a, D, K, τ_I, G) and a countermeasure at that quantity. SCAM **supports** the paper's choice of trigger rules over detection: if susceptibility is partly habitual, a fixed if-then response is more plausible than heightened vigilance. That is an argument by fit, not evidence for H12. Closest prior work: Ferreira, Coventry & Lenzini (2015) for the taxonomy; Cross & Gillett (2020) for the BEC framing of S2.

### 2.4 Security culture, descriptive norms and social influence

**What this line does.** In information-systems research, subjective norms and peer behavior predict employees' intention to comply with security policies, alongside perceived effectiveness and certainty of detection; severity of punishment unexpectedly had a negative effect (Herath & Rao 2009). Social proof works in security: telling Facebook users how many friends used a security feature raised exploration of that feature by 37 percent relative to a non-social announcement (Das et al. 2014). A workshop intervention based on participation, dialogue and collective reflection changed awareness and behavior indicators relative to a control group, at least in the short term (Albrechtsen & Hovden 2010). Interview work shows that employees resolve conflicts between security and work through peer trust, making non-compliance a collaborative act (Kirlappos & Sasse 2015). The long version builds on Cialdini, Reno & Kallgren (1990) and Edmondson (1999).

**Relation to Paper 2.** **Overlap, strong, for H18.** That norms and peer behavior shape security behavior is established in this literature, at least at the level of intentions and self-report. The long version's evidence row for H18 ("mechanism well supported, transfer untested") understates how much of the transfer already exists. **What remains:** (a) the specific contrast "descriptive norm predicts *verification of an action* better than individual knowledge *and better than the prescribed rule*"; (b) the modeling move that the norm sets the verification cost K, so that normalization attacks lower τ and raise K without changing any evaluation (7.5); (c) team-level measurement as the dependent variable. Kirlappos & Sasse (2015) is close to the paper's "groups as an attack surface" (7.6) and should be cited there. Albrechtsen & Hovden (2010) is the closest empirical precedent for "role and norm instead of appeal" (9.6, principle 7).

### 2.5 Bystander effect, diffusion of responsibility and reporting

**What this line does.** In an accounting experiment, managers with sole knowledge of a fraud were more likely to report it than when others also knew; the effect appeared only with strong evidence and was mediated by perceived personal responsibility (Brink, Eller & Gan 2015). In an IS-security survey of 431 respondents, evaluation apprehension and diffusion of responsibility inhibited bystanders' intention to act against colleagues' non-compliant mobile-device use (Paravastu, Simmers & Anandarajan 2018). Phishing-reporting research identifies individual predictors (self-efficacy, expected negative outcomes, self-monitoring) of reporting (Kwak et al. 2020), finds that reporting decreases as believability increases and that intending to report is not a subset of detecting (Kersten et al. 2022), and shows that crowdsourced reporting in a large organization is operationally feasible (Lain, Kostiainen & Čapkun 2022). The underlying mechanisms come from Darley & Latané (1968) and the meta-analysis by Fischer et al. (2011).

**Relation to Paper 2.** **Overlap, moderate, for H17 and V23.** The long version stated (former 7.9, now 7.10; 15.3) that diffusion of responsibility "has hardly been measured in security contexts"; v0.7 narrows this to verification coverage per action class and cites both studies, and its new Section 7.9 derives the n- and loss-dependence of coverage from the verification rule (volunteer's dilemma, Diekmann 1985). Brink, Eller & Gan (2015) and Paravastu, Simmers & Anandarajan (2018) are such measurements, one for fraud reporting and one for security bystander intention. The paper should cite them and narrow its claim: what is untested is the effect on **verification coverage of a specific action class** and the comparison "one named role versus two unnamed ones" (H17), not diffusion of responsibility in security as such. Brink et al.'s evidence-strength moderation is also relevant to the long version's limiting source (bystander effect weaker under high danger). **What is new:** the coupling to the verification rule (each verifier discounts by presumed verification of others) and the D argument for four-eyes approvals. Reporting research supplies an operationalization for the reporting-rate metric (9.7).

### 2.6 Risk compensation, over-reliance and responsibilization

**What this line does.** Risk compensation and risk homeostasis come from traffic safety (Peltzman 1975; Wilde 1982). In information security, a survey of 176 respondents integrated perceived technical protection into the theory of planned behavior and found a negative direct effect of perceived high technical protection on intention to comply with security policy, which the authors read as possible risk compensation; they recommend not exaggerating technical protection in training and emphasizing its limits (Zhang, Reithel & Li 2009). Automation research describes complacency and bias (Parasuraman & Manzey 2010). From the opposite side, Renaud et al. (2018) argue that "responsibilizing" individuals for cyber risk, as governments do, contributes to the success of attacks.

**Relation to Paper 2.** **Overlap, strong, for H16 and S22.** Zhang, Reithel & Li (2009) test essentially the mechanism of protection delegation (perceived protection lowers one's own compliance) in a security context, and their practical recommendation is close to S22 ("make visible what the institution does not catch"). The long version's evidence row for H16 ("no measurement of τ_I in security contexts known") must be revised. **What remains:** (a) the formal statement E′ = 1 − (1 − E)(1 − τ_I) that turns the attitude into a rationally applied rule with a miscalibrated parameter; (b) the claim that the calibration gap on A→I acts as a multiplier on all edges in the institution's area; (c) the derived tipping prediction V22 (from ΔE = u·Δa), which is not in the prior work found; (d) the stronger, still untested comparison in H16 (τ_I predicts click rates better than awareness test results). Renaud et al. (2018) frames the policy tension the paper must address: the paper does not argue for shifting responsibility to individuals; S20 assigns a named role inside the organization. This distinction should be stated.

### 2.7 Separation of duties, payment controls and extensions of the CIA triad

**What this line does.** Separation of duty and well-formed transactions are the core of the Clark–Wilson commercial integrity policy (Clark & Wilson 1987); separation of privilege appears among the design principles of Saltzer & Schroeder (1975); Zero Trust demands per-session verification (Rose et al. 2020). On security properties, the dependability taxonomy defines attributes such as reliability, availability, safety, integrity and maintainability, with security adding confidentiality (Avizienis et al. 2004). Parker (1998) extends the triad to six elements including utility and authenticity. Samonas & Coss (2014) argue that socio-technical extensions can be absorbed into a broader reconceptualization of the triad. The reference model of information assurance and security (RMIAS) consolidates the field's knowledge into one model and was evaluated with expert interviews, workshops and a case study (Cherdantseva et al. 2016). Standards differ on authenticity (ISO/IEC 27000:2018; NIST 2004 FIPS 199; BSI 2023). Trust research decomposes trustworthiness into ability, benevolence and integrity (Mayer et al. 1995) and, for automation, performance, process and purpose (Lee & See 2004).

**Relation to Paper 2.** **Overlap, strong, for the rules.** S1 to S4, S20, S21 and S24 are known controls (separation of duty, four-eyes, out-of-band call-back for payments). The long version says so itself (Chapter 14). **What is new** is the common justification from θ and the verification rule, the ranking by loss, and the observation that an unnamed or correlated second approver adds little (S20, S21). **Overlap, partial, for the matrix (C6).** Avizienis et al. (2004) already separate dependability attributes (reliability, safety) from security attributes; the paper's statement that competence and predictability are "close to safety" restates that split from the actor side and should cite it. Parker (1998) already supplies utility; Samonas & Coss (2014) and Cherdantseva et al. (2016) already extend or reframe the triad. **What remains:** placing trust dimensions on the actor (probability) axis and security objectives on the asset (loss) axis, the cell formula Risk_{k,z}, the reading of authenticity as an anchor beneath all rows, and the claim that competence has no CIA counterpart. One work found by title, Hoffman, Lawson-Jenkins & Blum (2006), "Trust beyond security: an expanded trust model", could be a direct precedent for linking trust and security properties; only its one-line abstract was available, so it is marked "to be checked" and must be read before submission.

## 5. Suggested condensed related-work section

> **Related work.** Field studies and meta-analyses of security awareness and phishing training find small or inconsistent effects on behavior. Embedded training did not make employees more resilient in a 15-month field study and works mainly as a reminder (Lain, Kostiainen & Čapkun 2022; Lain et al. 2024); annual training showed no measurable effect in a large randomized trial (Ho et al. 2025); meta-analyses report effects mostly on knowledge and attitudes, with weaker effects on measured behavior, and favor intensive, narrowly focused interventions (Bullee & Junger 2020; Prümmer, van Steen & van den Berg 2025). Usable-security research explains part of this through limited compliance budgets, rational rejection of advice and security fatigue (Beautement, Sasse & Wonham 2008; Herley 2009; Stanton et al. 2016), and the economics of security treats reliability as a public good prone to free riding (Varian 2004; Grossklags, Christin & Chuang 2008). Susceptibility models and persuasion taxonomies describe how attacks work on individuals (Vishwanath, Harrison & Ng 2016; Ferreira, Coventry & Lenzini 2015). Group-level findings exist but are scattered: norms and peer behavior predict security compliance intentions (Herath & Rao 2009), social proof raises engagement with security features (Das et al. 2014), diffusion of responsibility inhibits fraud reporting and bystander action (Brink, Eller & Gan 2015; Paravastu, Simmers & Anandarajan 2018), and perceived technical protection lowers intended compliance, consistent with risk compensation (Zhang, Reithel & Li 2009; Peltzman 1975; Wilde 1982). Separation of duty and out-of-band verification are established controls (Saltzer & Schroeder 1975; Clark & Wilson 1987; Cross & Gillett 2020), and the CIA triad has been extended several times (Parker 1998; Avizienis et al. 2004; Samonas & Coss 2014). This paper does not add a new control or a new effect. It adds a common derivation: from one evidence model it derives why verification, not evaluation, is the group-level bottleneck, why a miscalibrated assumed detection rate of the security institution switches off one's own verification, why verification should be triggered by the action class, and why trust dimensions and security objectives form a matrix rather than a mapping. All resulting hypotheses are untested.

(Approximately 360 words.)
