# Related Work: Engineering Calibrated Trust between Humans, Agents and Machines: Evidence Accounts, Decay and Re-verification

> Working document accompanying [paper.md](paper.md). Draft, 18 October 2026. Status markers as in the long version: (to be checked), (secondary citation), (verified YYYY-MM-DD); entries new in this document are additionally marked "new".

## 1. Scope and method

**Question.** For each contribution that Paper 3 claims, which prior work does the same or nearly the same, and what remains once that work is taken into account?

**Contributions examined.** They come from the paper and from long version Chapter 10, Sections 13.4, 13.6 and 13.8, H13, H14, H15c, V19 and rules S5, S6, S8 to S11, S13 and S19. The positioning table in Section 3 lists them.

**Sources and search.** The search ran on 18 October 2026. It used the Crossref REST API (bibliographic queries), OpenAlex (abstracts), Semantic Scholar (abstracts; rate-limited, and many abstracts were withheld by the publisher), arXiv abstract pages, the RFC Editor, publisher and DOI pages, and a general web search engine. Search terms combined: "trust in automation", "appropriate reliance", "overreliance" + "large language model"; "trust management", "PolicyMaker", "KeyNote", "SPKI", "macaroons", "attenuation", "delegation"; "zero trust", "continuous authentication", "risk-adaptive access control", "trust-based access control"; "remote attestation", "freshness", "RATS"; "trust and reputation" + "multi-agent"; "indirect prompt injection", "LLM agents" + "tool use" / "authorization" / "delegation", "agent-to-agent protocol"; "correlated errors" + "LLM", "LLM-as-a-judge", "self-preference"; "ISO/IEC 42001".

**Inclusion criteria.** Classic or widely used works per line, plus peer-reviewed or widely circulated work from 2015 to 2026 that touches a Paper 3 claim directly. A work was included only when its existence, authors, year, title and venue (and DOI where one exists) were confirmed on Crossref, OpenAlex, the publisher page, the RFC Editor or arXiv.

**Limits.** All 28 new references were read only at the level of the abstract, the publisher description or (for the two RFCs) selected sections. Their descriptions below therefore go no further than that material. All are marked "(new, secondary citation)". Five are arXiv preprints without a confirmed peer-reviewed venue (Debenedetti et al. 2025; Wallace et al. 2024; South et al. 2025; Ehtesham et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2025); they are cited as such. The search was not systematic in the PRISMA sense: one searcher, one day, no double screening. Industry white papers and vendor blogs on "zero trust for agents" turned up in the search but were not included. Workload-identity specifications (for example SPIFFE) could not be confirmed as a citable source with a year and publisher and are therefore not cited. The descriptions of works already in the long version follow the way the long version uses them; this document adds nothing to their content.

## 2. Lines of prior work

### 2.1 Trust in automation and AI, calibration and appropriate reliance

**What the line does.** People misuse and disuse automation (Parasuraman & Riley 1997), and monitoring declines even with reliable systems (Parasuraman & Manzey 2010). Lee & See (2004) argue that appropriate trust needs information about the performance, process and purpose of the system. Hoff & Bashir (2015; published online 2014) review 127 studies from 101 papers. They organize the factors that influence trust in automation into three layers: dispositional, situational and learned trust. They also name conditions under which trust and reliance diverge. Glikson & Woolley (2020) review trust in AI. Jacovi et al. (2021) treat human-AI trust as contractual: it is warranted only if actual trustworthiness causes it. Recent experiments on AI and LLM outputs test specific interventions. Showing confidence scores can help calibrate trust, but calibration alone did not improve joint decisions (Zhang, Liao & Bellamy 2020). Explanations reduce overreliance when the cost of engaging with them is low enough (Vasconcelos et al. 2023). First-person uncertainty expressions by an LLM reduced agreement with the system and increased accuracy in a preregistered experiment (S. Kim et al. 2024). Explanations increased reliance on both correct and incorrect LLM answers, while sources and inconsistent explanations reduced reliance on incorrect ones (S. Kim et al. 2025).

**Relation to Paper 3.** Paper 3 adopts the calibration problem and the Lee & See dimensions unchanged. It differs in two ways. First, it keeps trust in an agent per task type, as an evidence account with explicit uncertainty u and decay. Calibration then becomes the difference between a descriptive and a normative account rather than a match between subjective trust and measured reliability. Second, it assigns fluent wording a diagnosticity q close to 0. That point agrees with S. Kim et al. (2025): explanations raise reliance regardless of correctness. **Overlap.** H14 (people calibrate better when they see evidence per task type from verified results than when they see only the output) is close to the intervention studies above. Those studies already test what users see (confidence, explanations, sources, uncertainty wording). H14 differs only in the specific display: a verified track record per task type. A reviewer will ask why H14 is not simply a further display condition in that paradigm. The honest answer is that it is one, and that the model supplies a reason for this display condition. Hoff & Bashir's "learned trust" layer corresponds to the evidence account; their dispositional layer corresponds to the base rate. The paper should state this correspondence explicitly.

### 2.2 Trust management, capabilities and delegation that only narrows rights

**What the line does.** Blaze, Feigenbaum & Lacy (1996) frame the "trust management problem" as a distinct part of network security: formulating policies and credentials, checking whether credentials satisfy a policy, and deferring trust to third parties. Their PolicyMaker binds keys to the actions they are trusted to sign for, not to names. SPKI certificate theory (Ellison et al. 1999) defines authorization certificates. Its tuple-reduction rule intersects the authorizations and the validity periods along a delegation chain, so a chain can grant at most what each link grants. It also treats CRLs, revalidations and one-time revalidations as online validity conditions. Macaroons (Birgisson et al. 2014) are bearer credentials with chained MACs. Their caveats attenuate and confine when, where, by whom and for what purpose a request is authorized, and they support decentralized delegation. Least privilege goes back to Saltzer & Schroeder (1975). For agents, South et al. (2025, preprint) propose authenticated, authorized and auditable delegation to AI agents. Their framework extends OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect with agent-specific credentials and translates natural-language permissions into auditable access-control settings.

**Relation to Paper 3.** The machine-machine rules of Paper 3 adopt this line: "delegation may only narrow rights, never extend them" (table in long version 10.3), "rights passed on are at most as broad as one's own and shorter in time" (10.7), and short-lived, narrowly scoped tokens and capabilities as the counterpart of permission P. **Overlap: almost complete.** Narrowing delegation and time-bounded validity are formal properties of SPKI and macaroons. Paper 3 adds one thing: a reason for the narrowing. The discounting operator lets uncertainty only grow along a chain (long version 4.6), which motivates a limit on delegation depth (S13). That limit is not part of the SPKI or macaroon mechanism. Neither line of work couples the validity period of a credential to an evidence account about the holder's behavior; validity there is set by the issuer. This coupling is the residual contribution and should be stated as such.

### 2.3 Zero trust, continuous authentication and risk-adaptive access control

**What the line does.** Zero Trust Architecture (NIST SP 800-207) drops implicit trust based on network location; in the long version's reading, it requires identity to be re-verified before every session. Syed et al. (2022) survey zero trust tenets and the role of authentication and access control. They name "trust and risk computation techniques" among the open challenges. Continuous (active) authentication monitors users after initial access, mainly on mobile devices (Patel et al. 2016). Two older access-control models already compute permissions from a trust or risk quantity. TrustBAC (Chakraborty & Ray 2006) extends role-based access control with trust levels. Users are assigned to trust levels instead of roles, based on credentials, behavior history and recommendations, and the trust levels map to roles and permissions. Fuzzy MLS (Cheng et al. 2007) proposes risk-adaptive access control that treats the uncertainty and risk in each access decision explicitly.

**Relation to Paper 3.** The long version itself states that its principles "correspond to Least Privilege (Saltzer & Schroeder 1975) and Zero Trust (NIST SP 800-207)", and that what is new is "the coupling to an evidence account with temporal decay" (10.3). **Overlap: substantial and under-cited.** TrustBAC already derives permissions from behavior history; Fuzzy MLS already makes access decisions under explicit risk and uncertainty. Paper 3 differs in three points. (1) It separates trust b, distrust d and uncertainty u, so that a new or dormant node is "unknown", not "medium". (2) It uses a threshold from the loss and benefit of the action, θ = L/(L+N), with an uncertainty penalty κu. (3) It decays evidence, so that permissions lapse toward the base rate unless renewed. Whether TrustBAC or later trust-based access-control models include temporal decay could not be checked from the abstract. A reviewer from access control will know this line; the paper must cite it and narrow its novelty claim to points (1) to (3) and to the derived interval t*.

### 2.4 Remote attestation and workload identity

**What the line does.** Coker et al. (2011) define remote attestation as making a claim about properties of a target. Among their five principles is temporally fresh evidence. The IETF RATS architecture (Birkholz et al. 2023) defines the roles Attester, Verifier and Relying Party, with appraisal policies for evidence and for attestation results. It discusses how freshness is established (timestamps, nonces, epoch IDs) and judged against an expiry threshold in the appraisal policy. It also notes an unavoidable race: the attester's state may change right after evidence is produced. For AI agents, Huang et al. (2025, preprint) propose verifiable agent identities using decentralized identifiers and verifiable credentials. These identities capture capabilities, provenance, behavioral scope and security posture, together with dynamic fine-grained access control and consistent revocation.

**Relation to Paper 3.** Paper 3 adopts attestation as steps 1 to 4 of establishing trust (provider, model, runtime environment, version, configuration) and maps it to the base rate a. Its distinctive claim is that **attestation sets only the base rate; evidence r arises only from verified results; a correctly identified agent starts with u = 1.** RATS and Coker et al. treat attestation as evidence about state, not about behavior over time. Paper 3's separation of "who and what it is" (a) from "how it has performed" (r, s) is therefore a real distinction and complements this line. **Overlap.** The mapping "forgetting factor λ ↔ validity period of credentials, interval of re-attestation" (10.3) meets RATS freshness. There, too, an appraisal policy decides how old evidence may be. What Paper 3 adds is a formula for that age limit from u_max and the accumulated evidence n₀. The rule "a change of version or configuration turns the agent into a partly new node" (10.4) is close to the attestation idea that a changed measured state invalidates earlier results. The paper should say so.

### 2.5 Trust and reputation in multi-agent systems

**What the line does.** Computational trust is an old topic (Marsh 1994; Castelfranchi & Falcone 2010). The Beta Reputation System (Jøsang & Ismail 2002), on which the long version's evidence model rests, already contains a forgetting factor and reputation-based discounting of feedback. Subjective Logic provides the discounting operator (Jøsang 2016). Ramchurn, Huynh & Jennings (2004) survey trust in multi-agent systems and argue that the models all seek to minimize the uncertainty in interactions. TRAVOS (Teacy et al. 2006) computes an agent's trust in a partner with probability theory from past interactions. Without personal experience it draws on third-party reputation and handles the possibility that this reputation is inaccurate. Hoffman, Zage & Nita-Rotaru (2009) classify attacks on reputation systems by the component they target and survey defenses. Sybil attacks (Douceur 2002) are the canonical case.

**Relation to Paper 3.** **This is the closest prior work for the evidence account itself.** Evidence accounts with decay, discounting along referrals and robustness to inaccurate witnesses are established. Paper 3 adopts them explicitly. Its differences lie elsewhere: (a) it ties the account to a permission threshold per action class instead of partner selection; (b) it derives a re-verification interval from the decay; (c) it treats the account itself as an object of protection, with provenance per entry (S9, 10.7). Point (c) is in the spirit of the attack taxonomy of Hoffman et al. (2009). The paper must not present the decaying Beta account as its own contribution. The phrase "what is new is the coupling to an evidence account with temporal decay" (long version 10.3) is defensible only if "coupling to permissions" is the emphasis.

### 2.6 Security of LLM agents: injected instructions, tool use, excessive agency, agent protocols

**What the line does.** Greshake et al. (2023) show that LLM-integrated applications blur the line between data and instructions. Their indirect prompt injection places prompts in data that is likely to be retrieved, and it can manipulate which APIs are called. AgentDojo (Debenedetti et al. 2024) is an extensible evaluation environment for agents that run tools over untrusted data. It has 97 tasks and 629 security test cases. Its authors report that current LLMs fail many tasks even without attack, and that existing attacks break some security properties but not all. The instruction hierarchy (Wallace et al. 2024, preprint) attributes injections to models giving equal priority to developer, user and third-party text. It trains models to ignore lower-privileged instructions. CaMeL (Debenedetti et al. 2025, preprint) wraps the LLM in a system layer. The layer separates control and data flow, so untrusted data cannot change program execution, and uses capabilities to enforce policies on tool calls. OWASP (2025) lists Excessive Agency (LLM06) and Misinformation (LLM09). For agent-to-agent interaction, Ehtesham et al. (2025, preprint) compare MCP, ACP, A2A and ANP on discovery, communication patterns and security. A2A delegates tasks peer-to-peer using capability-based Agent Cards.

**Relation to Paper 3.** Paper 3's rule for the direction agent→human/content is this: **content from tools is data and carries no authority to instruct**; instructions are valid only from the authenticated principal via the designated channel; even authorized instructions are checked against the action class. In the model, an injected instruction is impersonation: the content claims the principal's edge. **Overlap: high for the rule.** The data/instruction separation is the core finding of Greshake et al. CaMeL implements it as a system design with capabilities, and the instruction hierarchy implements it as a training objective. What Paper 3 adds is (a) the reading of injection as an attack on edge attribution, which puts it in the same frame as CEO fraud and deepfake voice, and (b) the third check, correctness against the action-class threshold, which also applies to authorized instructions. Neither is a mechanism. It is a unifying justification, and the paper should claim only that. The pairing "Excessive Agency enlarges P; Misinformation is a loss in the utility column without any CIA violation" (long version 13.6) is an interpretation of OWASP categories. It is not found in OWASP itself.

### 2.7 Correlated verifiers: ensembles of the same model and LLM-as-judge

**What the line does.** LLM-as-a-judge is widely used. Zheng et al. (2023) report over 80% agreement between strong LLM judges and human preferences, together with position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases. Panickssery, Bowman & Feng (2024) find that LLM evaluators score their own outputs higher than humans would. They also find that self-recognition ability correlates linearly with this self-preference. E. Kim et al. (2025) evaluate more than 350 LLMs and find substantial error correlation (models agree 60% of the time when both err). Shared architecture and provider explain part of it, but larger and more accurate models have highly correlated errors even across architectures and providers. The authors examine the consequences for LLM-as-judge evaluation.

**Relation to Paper 3.** Paper 3 claims that two instances of the same model are correlated sources with D close to 1, that a cross-check counts as second evidence only with a different model, data basis or human verifier, and that many agreeing agents of the same origin count as one source (10.7, S6, H14 second part). The findings above support the first claim directly. **They also qualify the remedy.** If errors stay correlated across providers for capable models (E. Kim et al. 2025), "a different model, a different provider" (table 10.3, "diversity of verifiers") does not reliably reduce D. Paper 3 should cite this and weaken the wording: a different provider lowers D by an unknown and possibly small amount, and a human or a non-LLM check (test, reproducible result) remains the more independent verifier. The idea itself, counting correlated sources as one effective source via n_eff = n/(1+(n−1)D), is the long version's. Its application to model ensembles is not new in spirit, but the empirical anchor is now available.

### 2.8 AI risk frameworks that separate validity from security

**What the line does.** The NIST AI RMF (NIST 2023) presents "valid and reliable" as a basis of trustworthy AI and defines "secure and resilient" separately. It also notes that a ground truth sometimes does not exist or is not available (as used in long version 13.6). The EU AI Act, Art. 15, lists accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity as separate requirements for high-risk systems. ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (ISO/IEC 2023) specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining and continually improving an AI management system in organizations that provide or use AI-based products or services. Parker (1998) lists utility as a security property alongside the CIA triad.

**Relation to Paper 3.** H15c and S19 argue that utility becomes a risk dimension of its own for agents. The reason given is that reaching the target state is probabilistic per execution, so the risk moves from the project into operation. **Overlap: the separation itself is in the frameworks**, and the long version says so ("the AI frameworks themselves ... introduce the missing property separately"). What Paper 3 adds is the link to the trust model: competence has no counterpart in the CIA triad, utility is that counterpart, and it is verified per task type and per result rather than once per system. The paper should not suggest that the frameworks lack the distinction. Its claim is narrower: the distinction follows from the trust dimensions and has consequences for where evidence is kept. ISO/IEC 42001 could not be checked beyond its scope statement; whether it addresses validity monitoring per task remains open (to be checked against the text of the standard before any statement about its content).

## 4. Gaps: prior work the paper should engage with

- **Hoff & Bashir (2015).** The standard review of trust in automation after Lee & See. Its three layers (dispositional, situational, learned) map onto base rate, context and evidence account. Belongs in the human→agent section and in Related work.
- **Trust management systems: Blaze, Feigenbaum & Lacy (1996); Ellison et al. (1999, SPKI).** The term "trust management" and narrowing delegation with intersected validity periods originate here. Without them, the machine→machine rules read as if they were new. Belongs in the machine→machine section and the mapping table.
- **Macaroons (Birgisson et al. 2014).** A deployed form of attenuating, contextually confined credentials. The concrete counterpart of "narrowly scoped, short-lived tokens and capabilities". Belongs next to permission P in the mapping table.
- **Trust-based and risk-adaptive access control: Chakraborty & Ray (2006); Cheng et al. (2007).** They already derive permissions from behavior history or quantified risk. They are the strongest challenge to the claim that coupling permissions to trust is new. Belongs in Related work and in the discussion of H13.
- **Remote attestation: Coker et al. (2011); Birkholz et al. (2023, RATS).** Freshness and appraisal policies are the technical home of "λ ↔ re-attestation interval". Belongs in "Establishing trust" and the re-verification section.
- **Multi-agent trust and reputation: Ramchurn et al. (2004); Teacy et al. (2006); Hoffman et al. (2009).** Decaying, probabilistic trust between software agents and attacks on reputation systems are mature topics. Without them the evidence account looks new. Belongs in "Model in brief" (as origin) and in Related work.
- **LLM agent security: Greshake et al. (2023); Debenedetti et al. (2024, 2025); Wallace et al. (2024).** These are the primary sources for "content is data". AgentDojo also offers a ready benchmark for testing the agent→content rules (a possible addition to research phase 5, if the author adds it in the long version first). Belongs in the agent→content section.
- **Authorization of agents and agent protocols: South et al. (2025); Ehtesham et al. (2025); Huang et al. (2025).** Concrete proposals for delegating authority to agents and for agent identity. They are the closest current work on machine→machine and human→agent delegation. Belongs in the machine→machine section.
- **Correlated LLM errors and LLM-as-judge: Zheng et al. (2023); Panickssery et al. (2024); E. Kim et al. (2025).** These are the empirical basis for D close to 1 among model verifiers, and a reason to weaken "different provider" as a remedy. Belongs in the independent-verifiers rule and in H14 (second part).
- **Reliance on LLM outputs: Zhang et al. (2020); Vasconcelos et al. (2023); S. Kim et al. (2024, 2025).** These are the experimental paradigms in which H14 would be tested; they also show that explanations can increase reliance on wrong answers. Belongs in the human→agent section and in the operationalization of H14.
- **ISO/IEC 42001:2023.** It is the management-system standard for AI that a reader from governance will expect next to the NIST AI RMF and the AI Act. Its content beyond the scope statement is to be checked before it is used for any claim. Belongs in the utility section.
- **Not found or not confirmable in this search:** a peer-reviewed source that derives credential or re-attestation lifetimes from an explicit decay model. Its absence supports the t* contribution but does not prove that it is new; a targeted search in access-control venues (SACMAT, CCS, ESORICS) and the trust-management literature (IFIPTM) is still needed. Workload-identity specifications (SPIFFE/SPIRE) and OAuth token-lifetime guidance could not be confirmed as citable sources and should be added once a stable reference is available.

## 5. Suggested condensed related-work section

> Paper 3 builds on five lines of work. Research on trust in automation shows that people misuse and disuse automated systems (Parasuraman & Riley 1997), that appropriate trust requires information on performance, process and purpose (Lee & See 2004), and that trust has dispositional, situational and learned layers (Hoff & Bashir 2015); for AI, trust is warranted only when actual trustworthiness causes it (Jacovi et al. 2021; Glikson & Woolley 2020). Experiments with AI and LLM outputs show that confidence displays, explanations, sources and uncertainty wording change reliance, not always in the right direction (Zhang, Liao & Bellamy 2020; Vasconcelos et al. 2023; S. Kim et al. 2024, 2025). Computational trust models for agents keep probabilistic evidence with forgetting and discount third-party reports (Jøsang & Ismail 2002; Ramchurn, Huynh & Jennings 2004; Teacy et al. 2006). Our evidence account adopts them unchanged. Trust management and capability systems bind keys to permitted actions and let delegation only narrow rights and validity periods (Blaze, Feigenbaum & Lacy 1996; Ellison et al. 1999; Birgisson et al. 2014). Trust-based and risk-adaptive access control derives permissions from behavior history or quantified risk (Chakraborty & Ray 2006; Cheng et al. 2007). Zero trust removes implicit trust (NIST SP 800-207), and remote attestation requires fresh evidence judged against an appraisal policy (Coker et al. 2011; Birkholz et al. 2023). Work on LLM agents shows that retrieved content can act as instructions (Greshake et al. 2023) and proposes benchmarks and defenses that separate control from data (Debenedetti et al. 2024, 2025). Agent errors are correlated even across providers (E. Kim et al. 2025), and LLM judges favor their own outputs (Panickssery, Bowman & Feng 2024). AI risk frameworks separate validity from security (NIST 2023; EU AI Act Art. 15). Paper 3 adds no new mechanism to these lines. It links them through one quantity: permissions are tied to an evidence account that decays toward the base rate. From this it derives a re-verification interval per action class and a common justification for known rules. Attestation sets only the base rate. Independent verification is counted via the effective number of sources. Utility is treated as a separate risk dimension for agents. These derivations are untested.
